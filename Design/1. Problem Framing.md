# Problem Statement

## Domain

To understand the **senior sale**, you must first understand the beautiful chaos that is MIT's **dormspam**. Dormspam is an MIT tradition as old as the school itself. What started out as bulletin boards announcing club meetings in the central entrance of the Rodgers Building in Boston's Back Bay has evolved over the years into the mass emails we know today. The modern dormspam consists of announcements of every kind.

- Club meetings
- Party announcements for East Campus
- Open research positions
- Job postings
- Announcing seminars
- Advertising events on and around campus
- MIT administrative information
- Free food on campus
- Student deals on services
- Political and activism opportunities
- Lost and found items on campus
- Selling concert tickets or other single extra items
- Senior Sales
- And so much more!

The senior sale is a core element of MIT's dormspam culture. The name originates from seniors who, in moving out of the dorm, found themselves to have accumulated quite a lot of stuff that they just don't need. Rather than donating to thrift stores where an estimated 84% of clothing ends up in landfills anyway, seniors turned to selling their stuff as they move out. The small bit of cash they earn in the process doesn't hurt, but in general, senior sales are no great wealth generators, with most sales averaging less than a few hundred dollars in total across all items. It is also common for there to be free-with-purchase or even completely free items in senior sales in an attempt to just make sure things do not go to waste.

The sorts of things sold in senior sales are innumerable and unending, but some of the most common items include clothing & accessories, furniture & appliances like mini-fridges or AC units, and textbooks. Some personal favorites that I have seen in my tenure at MIT were a particularly beautiful dream catcher I still have hanging in my window and a kitty cat that is now my baby boy [Percy](Appendices/Pics/PXL_20251002_214930801.jpg). In the same way that the offerings of senior sales are ever expanding, the participants are expanding too. It is becoming more and more common for students of all ages to host "senior sales" of their own as a form of "spring cleaning."

The form of senior sales is pretty standard itself, with only slight variations. A seller creates a powerpoint with pictures of their items and puts their asking price, and size if applicable, next to them. They announce their senior sale on dormspam via an email with a link to their powerpoint. Typically, the first slide contains instructions, and these can vary but generally boil down to some form of venmo/zell me money and then put the word claimed somewhere on the slide. Sometimes the senior sales include an *"or best offer" (OBO)* feature where the seller will give the item to the best offer they get. Sometimes the pseudo-auction increases the price and sometimes decreases it, but these can be very messy to manage and often end up just going to the first satisfactory offer. Finally, the seller and buyer agree on a drop-off/pick up location that most often is a dorm lobby's delivery drop-off desk. Nearly every aspect of this format of the senior sale is messy and creates friction for either the buyer or the seller.

## Bad Situations

The current senior sale format has created a number of bad situations for both the buyers and sellers. The bad situations can be classified into a few key issues.

1. **Fragmented Discovery** - One of my friends wears clothing sizes that do not appear very often in senior sales. To find something that fits, she has to open senior sale emails individually, follow each link to a separate PowerPoint, and scan through unrelated furniture, appliances, and clothing hoping to find the occasional XL top or size 10 boot. She has mostly stopped looking because it is not worth sorting through so many sales for the chance of finding one "unicorn" item.
2. **Uncertain transaction states** - During my freshman year, my roommate was trying to buy an AC unit because our dorm does not have air conditioning. She opened a senior sale within minutes of the dormspam being sent, saw that the AC was still available, paid the seller, and thought she had successfully bought it. A few hours later, the seller refunded her because someone else had claimed it first. Because the PowerPoint, payment app, and seller's own records were separate, the item could appear available even when it effectively was not.
3. **Opaque price discovery** - One of my friends held an OBO senior sale because she had several nicer items and wanted to compare offers before selling them. However, every buyer messaged her independently, buyers had no idea whether their offers were competitive, and she had to keep track of all the offers herself. The communication became overwhelming enough that she usually ended up giving the item to the first person who made an offer she considered good enough.
4. **Features are split across many applications** - Sellers have to manage dormspam, a PowerPoint, Venmo or Zelle, direct messages, and pickup arrangements at the same time. When a seller has many items and buyers, keeping availability, payment, and communication synchronized becomes extra work and can lead to mistakes like selling the same item twice or forgetting to update its status.

## Corroboration

The senior sale is a niche part of MIT culture, so there is not much formal research about the problems with the system. However, there is outside evidence showing that senior sales happen at a large enough scale for these problems to matter.[An MIT Admissions article](https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/huge-dormspam-haul/) about dormspam describes students being "inundated with 200 senior sales in the last week of classes." DormCon also [specifically lists sales](https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/huge-dormspam-haul/) of furniture, clothing, and AC units as common uses of dormspam. Together, these support the idea that searching through many independent sales is not just a problem within my own friend group.
There is also evidence that MIT students value this kind of local reuse even if the current senior sale format is imperfect. [MIT's Infinite](https://news.mit.edu/2025/infinite-threads-helps-community-planet-popup-thrift-store-0606) Threads program collected and resold more than 1,000 pieces of clothing in the 2024–25 academic year, showing real demand for keeping unwanted items within the MIT community. My own experiences provide more specific examples of where the current system breaks down: one friend largely stopped browsing because finding items in her size took too much effort, my roommate repeatedly had AC purchases refunded after another buyer had already claimed them, and a senior seller abandoned her intended OBO process because managing the offers became overwhelming. At the same time, the fact that senior sales remain so common suggests that the system is not fundamentally broken; students clearly still value them. The problem is that the current process creates enough recurring friction to discourage some buyers and make sales unnecessarily difficult for sellers.

## Workarounds and Comparables

The current senior sale format is itself a workaround for existing online marketplaces. Students use dormspam, PowerPoint, Venmo or Zelle, and direct messaging because this combination preserves several qualities that matter in the MIT context: the audience is local, buyers and sellers are usually other students, many items can be advertised at once, and pickup can be as simple as leaving an item at a dorm desk. Sellers also use informal workarounds to address the system's weaknesses. Items are marked "claimed" directly on slides, OBO offers are tracked manually through messages, and word of mouth is sometimes used to bypass the discovery problem entirely. These workarounds keep senior sales functioning, but they also create the bad situations described above because information about availability, payment, offers, and pickup is spread across separate tools.

Comparable products include eBay, Craigslist, and Facebook Marketplace. While these platforms solve problems such as search, listings, and transaction tracking, a major part of what makes senior sales work is that they exist within the MIT community. Sellers generally want to know the person picking up an item is another MIT student, and many exchanges take place in dorms or dorm lobbies where access may be restricted to MIT community members. Buyers also benefit from the convenience of knowing that an item is likely only a short walk away rather than somewhere else in the Boston area. A general marketplace therefore loses some of the trust, convenience, and hyper-local feel that dormspam provides. The challenge isn't simply to create another resale marketplace, but to preserve the MIT-specific community and low-friction exchange that make senior sales appealing while improving the parts of the current process that make them hard to use.

## Solution sketch + feature adds

Keeping these problems in mind, I want to build a dedicated platform for senior sales. My vision is of a virtual marketplace that removes as much friction from this process as possible. Sellers would be able to create senior sales by uploading images and tagging them with descriptions (ie clothes, pants, top, appliances, pet, decoration, AC unit, tickets, graduation gear, textbooks, pickup location, condition, etc.) and sizes for clothing items. This would lend itself to an easily sortable/filterable marketplace for buyers to sift through instead of the endless PowerPoints.

I would also like to attach payment to the platform itself. However, I don't know if that is viable for the scope of this project so I am thinking of the following workflow for the payment:

1. Buyer clicks **"Claim"**
2. The app temporarily **reserves** the item for the buyer (marking it as **reserved** to other users) for a period of time. Perhaps 5-10 minutes
3. The app then displays the seller's Venmo QR code and Zelle account details
4. The buyer pays and marks as **paid** in the app
5. The app then marks the item as **pending seller confirmation**, and the seller receives a notification and, after confirming payment, clicks **payment received**.
6. The app marks the item as **claimed** and takes it down.

I could potentially incorporate a waitlist so that once an item is reserved, they can add themselves to a waitlist, and then they will be notified if the item becomes available again for any reason. For $0 items, the reservation steps could be skipped and go straight to claimed. For bundled items that are free with purchase, which can be a tag of its own, users should be able to select the free item they want as a multiple-choice option before claiming.

Another feature I would like to incorporate is a bidding function that allows sellers to designate items as OBO and then lets users participate in a traditional virtual auction. I also think a chat feature could allow buyers to ask the sellers questions or confirm drop-off/pickup details all in one place. In addition to the standard buyer and seller dashboards, I think allowing password-protected or group-specific senior sales could be useful because sometimes people want to offer items to their friends before offering them all up to the general public. Sellers could toggle a feature to make a sale private, which would generate a code buyers could enter in the marketplace to also see those private items, or just those private items with proper tagging and sorting.
